Mobile-O v2 Results Humans The wall Use it
Technical report

A 0.5B image model on a phone budget

Mobile-O v2 generates and edits images from a frozen 1.3B vision-language model and a 600M diffusion head, at a fixed 16×16 latent. This is a log of what actually moved the numbers — including the experiments that were measuring nothing, and the point where the remaining problem stopped being a training problem.

Model MiniCPM-V-4.6 → mcptf → SANA-600M → DC-AE Resolution 512px, 1024px decode Updated September 2026

The short version

  • Four of six targets are met by a single checkpoint at a single setting — a uniform weight soup of three RL runs. GenEval 0.902, ImageReward 0.957, GEdit 6.74, and DPG 82.2.
  • Weight soups beat every member they are made of, 7 times out of 7 — but only by about +0.07 on a 0–1 scale. A soup cannot manufacture a target its members miss by 0.15.
  • Fewer solver steps are better. Quality peaks at 8–12 steps, not 20. That is a 40% compute cut that improves human subjects, and it was found by measuring, not by tuning.
  • Two of our own human-quality rubrics were measuring the wrong thing and scored real photographs as worse than our generations. The fix was calibration against real photos, not a better prompt.
  • Faces are done. On paired tests against the real photographs they were edited from, two of our checkpoints are statistically indistinguishable from real. The remaining defect is held-object geometry.
  • The editing bottleneck is architectural. The model reproduces regions it should leave alone 2.64× worse than simply copying the source latent — because it never receives the source pixels.
  • 1024px costs +3 ms/image, because it is a decoder change, not a diffusion change.

01What the model is

Mobile-O v2 is a text-to-image and instruction-editing model assembled from parts that mostly already existed. A frozen MiniCPM-V-4.6 reads the prompt (and, when editing, the source image). A small learned connector maps its hidden states into the diffusion model's cross-attention space. A SANA-600M diffusion transformer denoises a 32-channel latent. A frozen DC-AE decodes it.

Only the middle two are trained: 600.6M parameters in 602 tensors. That is the entire shipped artifact — the released checkpoint is a head, not a model, and it pairs with stock weights for the other two.

The number that governs every decision below is 256. A 512px image is a 16×16 latent, which is 256 DiT tokens. This is a latency budget rather than a quality choice, and it is fixed. Most of the standard moves in image editing — concatenating the source image as extra tokens, diptych layouts, higher-resolution latents — buy quality by spending tokens, and are therefore unavailable. Several results below are shaped entirely by that constraint.

02Six targets, one scoreboard

The project was run against six fixed targets set at the start. They are not all equally hard, and treating them as one number hides the only interesting thing about them — that the checkpoints which win one tend to lose another.

Two notes on reading this. ImgEdit and GEdit are scored by a local Qwen2.5-VL-72B judge, not the GPT-4o leaderboard scale, so they are not comparable to published numbers. And the setting is part of the result: the same weights at a different guidance scale move by more than most of our training interventions did.

Why the columns fight each other

Raising guidance is the single strongest lever on prompt alignment: it moves GenEval, DPG and ImageReward together. It also moves FID the wrong way, and — as section 04 shows — it makes people look worse. There is no guidance scale at which all six columns are simultaneously satisfied, and most of the second half of this project was spent establishing that this is a property of the frontier rather than a tuning failure.

03What actually moved the numbers

Three things produced most of the gain, and none of them were new training data.

Fewer solver steps are better — by a lot, and for free

The protocol used 20 DPM-Solver++ steps because that is what the first eval script was written with. Sweeping it produced the most surprising result of the project: quality peaks around 8–16 steps and degrades toward 40. Against the 20-step protocol, 8 and 16 steps are both significantly better on human subjects (paired, 152 matched images, t = +2.09 and +3.10). Twenty-eight and forty steps are not significantly different from twenty — the extra compute buys nothing at all.

The lesson, not the number

This had been sitting in the default arguments the entire time. It was found by sweeping a parameter nobody had questioned, against a metric sensitive enough to resolve it. Every fine-tuning run in this project cost hours of eight-GPU time; this cost one afternoon of inference and beat most of them.

Guidance is the strongest lever and it points two ways

Classifier-free guidance moves GenEval, DPG and ImageReward together and moves FID and human quality against them. The table in section 02 shows the alignment side; here is the cost side.

This is also where one of the clearer process mistakes happened. An earlier recommendation of cfg 3.0 was made on the alignment targets alone, before its human quality had ever been measured. The measurement reversed the recommendation. A setting whose cost has not been measured is not a recommendation, it is a guess with a number attached.

Weight soups beat their own ingredients — within limits

Every reinforcement-learning run in the project starts from the same supervised checkpoint, which means they all live in one linearly-connected basin and can be averaged. Uniformly averaging three RL runs that each meet a different target produced soup3-targets, which beats all three of its members on DPG, ImageReward and GEdit simultaneously.

Across those seven soups the non-linear gain — the amount by which the soup exceeds the straight line between its endpoints — averaged about +0.07 on a 0–1 scale. That is a real, repeatable free lunch, and it is far too small to cover a target its members miss by 0.15. Soups consolidate; they do not rescue.

04Measuring humans, badly, twice

Halfway through, the goal changed to human faces and bodies. There was no metric for that, so we built one: HumanBench — a vision-language judge scoring six independent axes 1–5 on 38 prompts × 4 samples, with every axis independently nullable.

The first two versions were wrong, and the way they were wrong is the useful part.

Rubric v1 scored real photographs as worse than our generations

v1 rated real photographs at hands 2.946 / 51.2% defective, against our own generations' 2.798 / 60.8%. It also ranked deliberate hand close-ups — the category manual inspection found worst — as the best hands category, above full-body shots where hands are thirty pixels wide.

The confound is obvious once stated: v1 conflated "this hand is small, blurry or occluded" with "this hand is anatomically impossible". Photographs are full of the former. The axis was measuring hand size, not hand correctness.

v2 gated each axis on whether the feature was actually large enough to judge, and enumerated what does and does not count as a defect — "a photograph is allowed to be imperfect". It still failed all three of its pre-registered acceptance criteria. v3 added an objects axis, for reasons in the next paragraph.

A belief that survived four weeks and one measurement

The working assumption, from eyeballing contact sheets, was that hands in contact were the dominant failure — fused fingers in handshakes, fingers around a wine-glass stem. The judge disagreed. Re-reading the sheets showed why: those observations came from images generated at cfg 4.5, and at the shipping cfg 1.5 the defect is different. It is held-object geometry — mirror-duplicated mug handles, two-headed tools, a stem that merges into the hand. The objects axis exists because the metric corrected its author.

HumanBench is paired-only, and that is a finding

Absolute HumanBench scores failed calibration twice. What survives is the difference between two settings on identical prompts with identical noise — noise is seeded from zlib.crc32 of the prompt id, so image face_hard_06_2 is the same prompt and the same latent in every run. Two settings then differ only by the thing under test, and a paired test over 152 matched images resolves gaps that comparing two means cannot.

Faces are, in fact, finished

The question "how good are the faces?" has an absolute answer only against a ceiling. So we scored the real input photographs of an editing benchmark with the identical rubric, framing and judge — single image, no caption, never told they were generated.

05When two good metrics disagree

The obvious next step was a specialist metric. FaceScore is a reward model fine-tuned on pairs of high-quality versus synthetically degraded faces, purpose-built for exactly this. We ran the official implementation and weights over 5,000 FFHQ caption prompts, with identical noise per prompt across checkpoints. It reproduces the paper's own published example to four decimals, so the installation is right.

Two things to notice. First, all three soups beat the baseline by enormous margins. Second, and more interesting: every one of our checkpoints outscores real photographs, by a wide and highly significant margin.

This is not a broken metric; it is a metric measuring what it was trained to measure. FaceScore learned to separate clean faces from degraded ones, so it rewards the smooth, well-lit, idealized look that diffusion models drift toward under quality-reward RL. Real Flickr photographs have grain, motion blur, awkward lighting and unflattering angles, and score lower for it.

The quality-reward soups push further into that idealized region — which FaceScore rewards and which distributional distance to real faces penalizes, in the same order. So the honest statement is narrow: the soups make faces cleaner and more attractive; the baseline produces faces closer to the distribution of real photographs. Both are true, and which one you want is a product decision rather than a measurement.

Practical rule

A specialist metric earns the right to rank your checkpoints. It does not automatically earn the right to tell you which direction is better. Score the real data through the same pipeline; if real data does not win, you have learned something about the metric, not about the model.

06The wall is architectural

Editing was the target we never reached, and three separate attempts to fix it with data and losses returned nothing: pixel-aligned training pairs, a foreground mask, and a source-consistency loss. Three nulls in a row is usually a sign the hypothesis is wrong, so we measured instead of trying a fourth.

The probe is simple. At σ = 1 — pure noise, so the one-step prediction reflects only what the conditioning knows — how well does the model reproduce the cells an edit does not touch?

The cause is structural. The source image reaches the diffusion transformer only as the vision-language model's semantic tokens. The pixels are never there. The model is re-rendering the background from a description of it — which is exactly why no amount of aligned data, masking or consistency loss helped. None of them can supply information the input never carried.

The literature has converged on the same answer. Qwen-Image-Edit feeds the source through both Qwen2.5-VL for semantics and the VAE encoder for appearance. We only ever had the semantic half. The standard fix — concatenating the source as extra tokens, which the in-context editing line of work shows outperforms channel concatenation — doubles the token count, and our 256 tokens are a latency budget.

So the design under test adds the source without adding tokens: patch-embed the frozen VAE's source latent with the DiT's own patch size, and add it at block 0 behind a learned scalar gate. Token count, latency and the generation path are all untouched; a zero latent stays an exact no-op.

The experiment that was measuring nothing

That experiment had been run before and read as a null. It was not a null — it was a dead channel. The side channel was out = tanh(gate) × proj(z_src) with both factors zero-initialized, which is a zero-gradient fixed point:

d/d gate  = sech²(gate) · proj(z)  = 0    because proj is zero
d/d proj  = tanh(gate) · z         = 0    because gate is zero

The appeal of the double zero is real — it makes step 0 bit-identical to the starting checkpoint. But only one factor needs to be zero for that, and zeroing both creates a saddle the optimizer cannot leave. The run trained for 487 steps with tanh(gate) printing exactly +0.0000.

The dangerous part is not the bug, it is that a dead channel scores identically to its own control. A 31-hour run would have completed, produced a number matching the baseline, and been written up as "the architecture idea does not work". Three guards now exist: the projection is seeded from the DiT's own patch-embedding weight, a smoke test asserts the projection is non-zero, and training crashes if the gate is still exactly zero after 200 batches.

This is a process result, not a quality result. A rising gate proves the channel is used, not that preservation improved. That is decided by the source-preservation L1 against two already-measured controls, and by re-running the 2.64× probe. Those numbers are not in yet.

071024px for three milliseconds

The 256-token budget rules out generating at a 32×32 latent: it would roughly quadruple the transformer cost, which is the entire thing the budget exists to prevent. So 1024px is produced after the diffusion model instead.

The frozen DC-AE decoder emits its last hidden features — 128 channels at 512px, the tensor that normally feeds conv_out — and a trained 106M head maps those directly to 1024px RGB, anchored on a bicubic upsample of the ordinary 512px decode. The diffusion transformer and the autoencoder are both untouched.

Rendering the same prompt and seed at both sizes and downscaling the 1024 result back to 512 differs from the native 512px output by a mean of 1.0/255. It is the same image, decoded better — not additional diffusion detail, and it should not be described as such.

We chose this over the alternative — a SwinIR upsampler widening the latent 16×16 to 32×32 before the frozen decode — on a reconstruction eval: FID 1.76 vs 2.077 and OCR 47.9 vs 40.

08Using it

The released checkpoint is the trained head only: 602 tensors, 548 in the diffusion transformer and 54 in the connector. The vision-language model is frozen during training, so it is not duplicated — it and the autoencoder are fetched from their own repositories on first run.

git clone https://github.com/ahmedheakl/mobileov2-minicpm
cd mobileov2-minicpm
pip install -r requirements.txt

# transformers >= 5 is required: MiniCPM-V-4.6 is a native
# transformers model and the import fails on 4.x
python infer.py \
  --prompt "a woman holding a ceramic mug in a sunlit kitchen" \
  --out out/gen.png

# several prompts at once -- --out becomes a directory.
# batch them: the first call pays ~2s of CUDA warm-up
python infer.py --prompt "a cat" "a dog" --out out/
python infer.py \
  --image photo.jpg \
  --prompt "make it snow" \
  --out out/edit.png
python infer.py --size 1024 \
  --prompt "a man repairing a bicycle wheel" \
  --out out/big.png

# works for editing too; the 426MB decoder head
# downloads once from the same repository

Defaults are 12 steps at cfg 1.5. Both look low and neither is an oversight — section 03 is the argument for the first and section 03's guidance chart for the second. Raising either improves a benchmark column and makes the pictures worse.

09What is still open

DPG ≥ 85 was never reached by anything. The record across 121 measured settings is 84.61, and stock SANA-600M reaches 84.04 without any of our training, so the remaining 0.4–1.0 is not obviously ours to win. Two separate confident explanations for the ceiling — first that it saturates, then that the increments were constant and 85 was reachable — were both wrong, and both were shape-fitting inside a 0.78-point noise floor.

FID ≤ 8 with GenEval ≥ 0.9 remains unsolved, but not for the reason we assumed for months. Stock SANA-600M scores 7.264 and our flow-matching-only run hit 5.955 through the identical scorers, so the envelope contains the target. The loss is in the conditioning path and in reinforcement learning, which gives back roughly 3.3 FID on a good supervised checkpoint. The next attempt is representation alignment, which is a training-time loss only — it adds no tokens, no parameters at inference and no latency. Our 16×16 latent is 256 tokens and DINOv2 ViT-B/14 at 224px is exactly 256 patch tokens, so the alignment is one-to-one with no pooling.

ImgEdit ≥ 3.5 waits on the source-latent experiment in section 06.

Six things worth taking away

  1. Calibrate against real data. Two rubrics and one specialist metric all ranked real photographs below our generations. Every one of those was caught by scoring real data through the same pipeline, and none would have been caught by reading the outputs.
  2. A mechanism that is a no-op at initialization must be shown to leave the no-op. A dead channel is indistinguishable from a refuted hypothesis.
  3. Pair everything. Fixed seeds keyed on the prompt turn a noisy comparison of two means into a paired test that resolves differences an order of magnitude smaller.
  4. Know your noise floor before claiming a trend. GenEval below 0.019, DPG below 0.78 points, ImgEdit below 0.05 — several confident stories here were fitted to movement inside those bands.
  5. Sweep the parameters nobody questions. The single best quality-per-compute result was a default argument that had never been examined.
  6. Three nulls in a row means measure, not iterate. One probe explained all three and redirected the work from data and losses to architecture.
