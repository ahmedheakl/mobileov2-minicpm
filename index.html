Mobile-O v2 is a small model that creates images from text and edits images from written instructions. It's the second version of Mobile-O, and like the first one, it's built with phones in mind. The diffusion model that draws each image works on only 256 tokens, and every change we made kept it that way.

v2 pairs an efficient vision-language model (VLM), which stays frozen, with an efficient diffusion transformer (DiT), for a total of 1.9B parameters. It makes a 512×512 image in about 0.8 seconds on one desktop GPU.One NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000, one image at a time, 20 sampler steps. Compared with v1, it follows prompts more closely, handles long prompts better, makes more realistic images and edits images much more reliably. On GenEval it also beats Janus and JanusFlow, two slightly larger models that can both understand and generate images, and it is ahead of both on all three understanding benchmarks in Figure 1.

What's new in v2

  • Generation and editing in one model. The same weights create images from text and edit existing images from instructions.
  • A stronger reader. A frozen, efficient VLM reads the prompt, and the source image when editing. We only train a small connector and the DiT.
  • Unified RL. One reinforcement learning (RL) run improves generation and editing together. Its rewards target object presence, counting, long-form prompts, image editing and text-rich images.
  • 1024×1024 output for 7% more time. A small add-on to the image decoder outputs four times the pixels without changing the diffusion model.

How it works

Mobile-O v2 has four parts. Two of them stay frozen, and we train the other two.

The VLM reads the input. It reads the prompt, and when editing it also looks at the source image. We use its last hidden layer as the description of what to draw. It stays frozen, so it keeps all of its ability for image understanding.

The connector translates. A small network turns that description into the form the DiT expects.

The DiT draws. It starts from random noise and removes it step by step until a small 16×16 latent image is left. We fine-tune all of it.

The decoder makes the pixels. A frozen decoder turns the 16×16 latent into a 512×512 image.

The token count shaped most of the design. A 512×512 image becomes a 16×16 latent, which the DiT processes as 256 tokens. Its cost grows with the number of tokens, and on a phone that decides how long you wait, so every improvement in this post had to work without adding tokens.

Results

We ran v1 and v2 through the same evaluation code, both with 20 sampler steps and guidance 1.5. v2 is ahead on every benchmark we ran, and its biggest GenEval gains are in the three categories where v1 was weakest: counting, putting objects in the right position, and giving each object the right color.

The images themselves also look better. v2's FID fell from 23.3 to 13.5, and it beats both sizes of v1 on every image-quality measure we ran.

Training

We trained v2 in two stages. Supervised fine-tuning teaches the model from examples, and reinforcement learning then improves it using rewards.

Learning from examples

We first pretrain the connector and the DiT on text-image pairs. We then fine-tune them on image generation, then on editing, and finally on an even mix of the two, so that one model learns both tasks without either one crowding out the other.

Learning from rewards

For the second stage we use GRPO, adapted to diffusion models as in Flow-GRPO. For each prompt the model makes a group of 12 images, and we score each one. Training then moves the model toward the images that scored above their group's average. The VLM stays frozen, so each prompt only has to be read once per group.

Generation and editing prompts share the same training run, which is why we call it unified RL. Each image gets the rewards that fit its task.

  • Objects. A detector checks for the right objects with the right counts, colors and positions, and gives partial credit.
  • The whole prompt. A frozen Qwen3-VL-8B scores how likely the full prompt is, given the image.This reward comes from "Read It Back: Pretrained MLLMs Are Zero-Shot Reward Models for Text-to-Image Generation" (arXiv 2607.11886). This works for long, detailed prompts that the detector can't check.
  • Edits. A judge model scores whether an edit followed the instruction, kept the rest of the image consistent, and looks good.
  • Text. An OCR model reads the words in images whose prompts ask for text.

These rewards come on very different scales. We standardize each one within its own group of samples before adding them up, so that no single reward dominates. After 500 steps of this training, GenEval rose from 0.84 to 0.91, DPG-Bench from 79.1 to 80.7 and ImageReward from 0.65 to 0.76. Both editing benchmarks went up as well, ImgEdit from 2.99 to 3.09 and GEdit from 6.38 to 6.52.During development we score edits with a local Qwen2.5-VL-72B judge, which gives lower scores than GPT-4o. That is why these ImgEdit numbers are lower than the GPT-4o score in Figure 1 (3.7).

1024×1024 output

To generate a 1024×1024 image directly, the diffusion model would need a 32×32 latent, which means four times as many tokens and roughly four times the work. So we add the extra pixels afterwards. A small network, which we call the 1024px head, reads the decoder's last internal features and adds fine detail on top of a plain 2× upscale of the 512px image.

The diffusion model doesn't change, and neither does the content of the image, so our benchmark scores stay the same at 1024px. Figure 8 shows what it costs in time.

Portraits

Small mistakes are easiest to see on faces, so we end with twelve portraits made by v2. They cover a range of ages, skin tones and lighting, from a baby on a parent's shoulder to a man in a dark coat against a black background.These portraits use 12 sampler steps instead of the 20 used elsewhere in this post, which gives skin a more natural texture.
