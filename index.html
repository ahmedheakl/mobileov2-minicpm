Mobile-O v2: generating and editing images on a phone budget

A frozen 1.3B vision-language model, a small learned connector, and a 600M diffusion transformer at a fixed 256-token latent. This is how we took it from supervised training through multi-task reinforcement learning, model merging and a 1024px decoder — with every number measured, including the ones that went the wrong way.

Text-to-image + instruction editing Trained 600.6M params Output 512px, or 1024px via decoder head

What we contribute

  • A unified generator and editor at a mobile latency budget. Against Mobile-O v1 on the same scorers: GenEval 0.792 → 0.912, DPG 73.9 → 80.7, FID 23.3 → 13.5.
  • Multi-task GRPO with dense rewards. A detector-only reward reward-hacked: GenEval rose to 0.95 while DPG fell from 72.6 to 66.0. Scoring the full caption with a VLM's log-likelihood, and standardizing each reward within its own task pool before mixing, fixed it — and lets one RL run train generation and editing together.
  • A 1024px decoder head that reads the frozen VAE decoder's features: 4× the pixels for +7% latency, content-neutral, FID −0.5. The diffusion model never sees more tokens.
  • Model merging that beats its ingredients. Averaging three RL models, each strong on a different target, beats all three on DPG, ImageReward and GEdit at once — and meets three of six targets.
  • Faces at the real-photo ceiling. On paired tests against the photographs they were edited from, our faces are statistically indistinguishable from real ones.
  • A measured diagnosis of the editing gap: the model reproduces regions it should leave alone 2.64× worse than copying the source — because the source pixels never reach it.

01Architecture

Mobile-O v2 is assembled from a strong frozen reader and a small trained writer. A frozen MiniCPM-V-4.6 reads the prompt — and, when editing, the source image. A learned connector turns its last hidden layer into the diffusion model's cross-attention space. A SANA-600M diffusion transformer denoises a 32-channel latent, and a frozen DC-AE decodes it. The number that governs every design decision is 256. A 512px image is a 16×16 latent, which is 256 transformer tokens. That is a latency budget, not a quality setting, and it is fixed. Most of the standard moves in image editing — appending the source image as extra tokens, diptych layouts, larger latents — buy quality by spending tokens, so they are off the table here. Several results below are shaped entirely by that constraint.

Only the middle two components are trained. The released artifact is therefore a head, not a model: 602 tensors that pair with stock weights for the vision-language model and the autoencoder, which download from their own repositories on first run.

Why no single setting wins every column

Guidance is the strongest lever on alignment: raising it moves GenEval, DPG and ImageReward together. It moves FID the wrong way, and — section 07 — it makes people look worse. The two reference rows matter most: stock SANA-600M, the same diffusion transformer we train, reaches FID 7.26 through our scorers, so the FID target is inside the architecture's envelope. What costs us FID is the recipe — the conditioning path, and reinforcement learning.

04Multi-task GRPO with dense rewards

Supervised training gets the model to follow instructions; reinforcement learning is what gets it to follow them precisely. We use GRPO adapted to flow matching: at each step, sample a group of 12 images per prompt with a stochastic sampler, score them, and push the policy toward the better-than-average ones. Only the connector and diffusion transformer are updated; the vision-language model and the autoencoder stay frozen, so each prompt is encoded once and reused across the whole group.

The first reward design reward-hacked

Our first runs used the obvious reward: GenEval's own object detector, which checks whether the right objects appear in the right counts, colours and positions. It worked exactly as specified — and that was the problem.

The detector can only score short, structured prompts — "three red apples" — because it needs metadata to know what to look for. It is structurally blind to a paragraph describing lighting, texture and spatial layout, which is what DPG-Bench and PRISM measure. So we redesigned the reward.

What we changed

Dense, not binary
The GenEval detector returns a continuous score from mask and CLIP confidences instead of pass/fail, so every sample in the group carries gradient.
Read it back
A frozen Qwen3-VL-8B scores the mean log-likelihood of the full prompt given the image. It needs no metadata, so it scores paragraph prompts directly.
Standardize per pool
Rewards live on different scales — [0,1] versus an unbounded log-likelihood. Each is standardized within its own task pool across all GPUs before mixing, never summed raw.
One run, two tasks
Generation and editing prompts share a single RL run with task balancing, plus an OCR edit-distance reward for text and a judge-based reward for edits.

Two rules the RL work taught us

The online reward curve is not the benchmark. Twice, a run whose reward was climbing produced a checkpoint that was worse on held-out evaluation. Only checkpoint evals settle a claim. And RL goes after supervised training, never before it: fine-tuning an RL checkpoint with SFT regressed all three times we tried it.

05The 1024px decoder head

At 256 tokens, generating at a 32×32 latent would roughly quadruple the transformer's cost — the one thing the budget exists to prevent. So we produce 1024px after diffusion instead, and compared two ways of doing it.

Approachwhere it actsparamscontent-neutral?
Latent upsamplerSwinIR-style, 16×16 → 32×32 latent, before the frozen decoder97.5Mno
Decoder head · shippedreads the frozen decoder's 128-ch features at 512px, maps them to 1024px RGB106.5Myes

The decoder head taps the tensor that normally feeds the decoder's final convolution — its richest spatial representation — and learns a residual on top of a bicubic ×2 of the ordinary 512px image. Starting from the bicubic anchor means it begins as a no-op and only learns to add detail. The autoencoder and diffusion transformer are both untouched.

06Model merging

Every RL run in the project starts from the same supervised model, so they all sit in one linearly-connected region of weight space and can simply be averaged. We trained three RL variants, each good at a different target, and took their uniform average.

The limit matters as much as the effect. Across those seven merges the gain above the straight line between endpoints averaged about +0.07 on a 0–1 scale. That is a real and repeatable free lunch, and far too small to cover a target the members miss by 0.15. Merging consolidates strengths; it does not create one that no member has.

07The inference recipe

Two settings nobody had questioned turned out to matter more than most of the training runs.

Fewer solver steps are better

The evaluation protocol used 20 DPM-Solver++ steps because the first script did. Sweeping it, on a metric we built for human subjects (section 09), showed quality peaking at 8–16 steps. All four of those settings are significantly better than 20, paired over 152 matched images with identical noise; 28 and 40 steps are not.

Guidance trades alignment for people

Classifier-free guidance is the strongest single lever on GenEval, DPG and ImageReward. It has a cost that no alignment benchmark sees.

A third lever is free: interval guidance, applying guidance only for the early, high-noise part of sampling. On the RL model at cfg 3.0 it cuts FID from 15.40 to 14.37 while GenEval moves 0.013 — inside the 0.019 noise floor — and DPG rises slightly.

A mistake worth naming

We once recommended cfg 3.0 on the strength of the alignment benchmarks, before its effect on human subjects had been measured. The measurement reversed it. A setting whose cost has not been measured is not a recommendation; it is a guess with a number attached.

09People and faces

Sixteen prompts built to be hard for faces — wrinkles, freckles, open mouths, glasses reflecting a window, hair across the face, two faces cheek to cheek, deep shadow. All four seeds are shown for every prompt; switch between them. Nothing here is selected.

We needed a metric, and built one badly twice

There was no benchmark for anatomical correctness, so we built HumanBench: a local Qwen2.5-VL-72B judge scoring six independent axes 1–5 — hands, held objects, mouth, face, body, realism — on 38 prompts × 4 samples, with every axis allowed to return "not visible" rather than guess.

The first version scored real photographs as worse than our generations: hands 2.946 and 51.2% defective for real photos, against 2.798 and 60.8% for ours. It had conflated "this hand is small, blurry or occluded" with "this hand is anatomically impossible", and photographs are full of the former. It was measuring hand size. The second version gated each axis on whether the feature was large enough to judge — and still failed all three of its pre-registered checks.

What survived is narrower and more useful: HumanBench is paired-only. Noise is seeded from a hash of the prompt id, so an image is the same prompt and the same latent in every run, and two settings differ only by the thing under test. A paired test over 152 matched images resolves gaps that comparing two means cannot. Every human-quality number in this report is a paired difference.

The judge corrected us

From contact sheets, we were sure the dominant failure was hands in contact — fused fingers in handshakes. The judge disagreed. Those sheets had been generated at cfg 4.5; at the shipping cfg 1.5 the defect is different — held-object geometry: mugs with two handles, tools with two heads, a stem merging into a hand. The objects axis exists because the metric was right and we were not.

Faces are at the real-photograph ceiling

An absolute face score only means something against real photographs. So we scored the real input photographs of GEdit's human-retouching subset through the identical rubric and judge — single image, no caption, never told they were generated — and paired our edits of those same photographs against them.

When a specialist metric and a distribution disagree

We also ran FaceScore, a reward model fine-tuned on pairs of high-quality versus degraded faces, over 5,000 FFHQ caption prompts with identical noise per prompt. The official implementation reproduces the paper's own published example to four decimals.

This is not a broken metric; it measures what it was trained to measure. FaceScore learned to separate clean faces from degraded ones, so it rewards the smooth, well-lit, idealized look that quality-reward RL pushes toward. Real photographs have grain, blur and awkward light, and score lower for it. The honest statement is narrow: the merged models make faces cleaner; the RL model makes faces closer to real photographs. Which one you want is a product decision, not a measurement.

10The editing wall is architectural

Editing is the target we have not reached, and three separate attempts to fix it with data and losses all returned nothing: pixel-aligned training pairs, a foreground mask, and a source-consistency loss. Three nulls in a row usually means the hypothesis is wrong, so instead of trying a fourth we measured.

The probe: at pure noise, the model's one-step prediction reflects only what its conditioning knows. How well does it reproduce the regions an edit is supposed to leave alone?

What the model is givenreconstruction error ↓variance explained
the real source image0.4690.55

no model — just copy the source latent0.1780.83

The cause is structural. The source image reaches the diffusion transformer only as the vision-language model's semantic tokens. The pixels are never there, so the model re-renders the background from a description of it — which is exactly why aligned data, masking and consistency losses did nothing: none of them can supply information the input never carried.

The field has converged on the same answer. Qwen-Image-Edit routes the source through both its vision-language model for semantics and the VAE encoder for appearance. We only had the first half. The usual way to add the second — appending the source as extra tokens — doubles our 256-token budget. So the experiment in flight adds it without adding tokens: the source's VAE latent is patch-embedded with the transformer's own patch size and added at block 0, behind a learned scalar gate. Latency and the generation path are unchanged. It is the dashed path in the figure in section 01.

The experiment that had been measuring nothing

This had been attempted before and read as a null. It was not a null; the channel was dead. It was built as tanh(gate) × proj(z_source) with both factors initialized to zero — a fixed point with no gradient:

∂/∂gate = sech²(gate) · proj(z)  =  0     because proj = 0
∂/∂proj = tanh(gate) · z         =  0     because gate = 0

Zeroing one factor makes step 0 identical to the starting model, which is the point. Zeroing both creates a saddle the optimizer cannot leave. The run trained 487 steps with the gate printing exactly +0.0000. The danger is not the bug: a dead channel scores identically to its own control, so a 31-hour run would have finished and been written up as "the idea does not work".

The fix seeds the projection from the transformer's own patch-embedding weights, keeps the zero on the scalar gate alone, and crashes the run if the gate is still exactly zero after 200 batches.

11Use it

Everything downloads on first run — the trained head from Hugging Face, and the frozen vision-language model and autoencoder from their own repositories.

git clone https://github.com/ahmedheakl/mobileov2-minicpm
cd mobileov2-minicpm
pip install -r requirements.txt

# transformers >= 5 is required: MiniCPM-V-4.6 is a native
# transformers model, and the import fails on 4.x
python infer.py --prompt "a red fox in a snowy meadow at dawn" --out out/fox.png

# batch prompts: the first call pays ~2 s of CUDA warm-up
python infer.py --prompt "a cat" "a dog" "a lighthouse" --out out/
python infer.py --image photo.jpg --prompt "make it snow" --out out/edit.png
python infer.py --size 1024 --prompt "a lighthouse at dusk" --out out/big.png

# the 426 MB decoder head downloads once; works for editing too
python infer.py --size 1024 --image photo.jpg --prompt "make it autumn" --out out/big_edit.png

Defaults are 12 steps at cfg 1.5. Both look low and neither is an oversight — section 07 has the argument for each. Raising either improves a benchmark column and makes the pictures worse.

12What is still open

DPG ≥ 85. The record is 84.61 across every setting we measured, and stock SANA-600M reaches 84.04 without any of our training, so the last point is not obviously ours to win. Two confident explanations of the ceiling — first that it saturates, then that the gains were constant and 85 was reachable — were both wrong, and both were curve-fitting inside a 0.78-point noise floor.

FID ≤ 8 with GenEval ≥ 0.9. The architecture reaches FID 7.26 unconditioned by our training, and a flow-matching-only run of ours hit 5.96 — so the target is inside the envelope, and what costs it is the conditioning path and RL. Next is representation alignment: a training-only loss that aligns an early transformer block with DINOv2 features. It fits unusually cleanly — our 16×16 latent is 256 tokens and DINOv2 ViT-B/14 at 224px is exactly 256 patch tokens — and it adds no tokens, parameters or latency at inference.

ImgEdit ≥ 3.5 waits on the source-latent experiment in section 10.

Six things we would tell ourselves at the start

  1. Score real data through your own metric. Two rubrics and one published metric all ranked real photographs below our generations. Every one was caught that way, and none would have been caught by looking.
  2. Anything that is a no-op at initialization must be shown to leave the no-op. A dead channel is indistinguishable from a refuted idea.
  3. Pair everything. Seeding noise on the prompt turns a comparison of two noisy means into a paired test that resolves differences an order of magnitude smaller.
  4. Know the noise floor before you see a trend. GenEval under 0.019, DPG under 0.78, ImgEdit under 0.05 — several stories here were fitted to movement inside those bands.
  5. Sweep the defaults nobody questions. The best quality-per-compute result in the project was a default argument.
  6. After three nulls, measure instead of iterating. One probe explained all three and moved the work from data and losses to architecture.
